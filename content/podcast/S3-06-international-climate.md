**Glenn**<br>
Today we're talking about climate diplomacy and international climate negotiations.
 
Back in the 1970s, some of us can remember that there was a problem with a growing hole in the ozone layer, caused by chlorofluorocarbons, or CFCS, in aerosols and refrigerants.
 
This came to somewhat of a head in 1985, when researchers confirmed the existence of a large seasonal ozone hole over Antarctica. That same year, governments adopted the Vienna Convention for the Protection of the Ozone Layer, creating a framework for international cooperation. Just two years later, nations negotiated the Montreal Protocol, which committed countries to phase out the production and consumption of ozone depleting substances, including CFCS. Subsequent timely action accelerated the phase out of harmful chemicals and saw the ongoing recovery of the ozone layer.
 
It set the tone for later climate agreements.
 
Then in 1992, at the Rio Earth Summit, governments adopted the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change and established the principle that countries would work together to prevent dangerous human interference with the climate system. The world had done it once; surely it could do it again, and quickly.
 
Janaline, what's happened since 1992 in the world of climate diplomacy? How are things going?
 
**Janaline**<br>
Well, a lot has happened since 1992 in climate diplomacy, not least the growing and visible impacts of climate change.
 
Post Second World War, with the creation of the United Nations, the reaction to two devastating wars was ‘countries need to get together and cooperate’. In 1972, there was the Stockholm Conference on the Human Environment, which set the frame for the idea that countries could cooperate on transboundary environmental issues, and at that stage people were talking about pollutants.
 
At the Rio Conference in 1992, it was the 20th anniversary of the Stockholm Conference. It was the United Nations Conference on Environment and Development: importantly, the concept of sustainable development had started to get a bit of traction. On climate change, it was very much a sense that: the world can get together, set a bunch of rules like they did with the ozone depleting substances, and the climate would be saved.
 
The difference between the Montreal Protocol and the Convention on Climate Change was that the Montreal Protocol was dealing with a very, very small list of discrete substances that were being produced for very specific purposes, basically refrigeration and air conditioning. Commercial interests involved were very, very small.
 
Climate change was dealing with the emission of carbon dioxide. It was dealing with the entire basis of the post-industrial revolution economy and so there were massive interests involved.
 
There was a huge debate about the relative responsibility that should be shouldered by developed countries and developing countries. And the argument ran: ‘the Europeans and the North Americans, principally, have created this problem by industrialising. And now they're telling all of these developing countries and these emerging economies in Asia and Africa and Latin America that they are not allowed to develop to this stage’. And there were all these conspiracy theories about how this was actually just the new colonialism: having lost the decolonisation fight, all of the old imperial powers were now trying to stop the development of their former colonies.
 
Meanwhile, the climate is changing. And I think in 1992, this was a very intellectual argument. It was about, well, you know, if climate changes, we'll see sea level rise and we'll see this and we'll see that. And everybody said, ‘yes, yes, that's all very interesting’.
 
In the 2010s, we start to see actual impacts of climate change. And then we come to the 2020s, where we start having regular catastrophic bushfires. Every year since about 2015 has been the hottest year on record. Once-in-a-hundred-year floods are happening every two years. Physics and biology are now telling us that actually climate change was real.
 
All of those doomsayers in the 1990s saying ‘you talk about the economic cost of shifting away from a fossil fuel based economy, but you're not taking into account the billions and billions of dollars of damage that climate change is going to cause’. And now we're in the billions and billions of dollars of damage that climate change is causing.
 
The other thing that I think has changed is that, in the 1990s, there weren't really a lot of good options to replace fossil fuels. So unless you were willing to go into a massive deindustrialisation program across the world - and I'm not going to lie, there were people who were saying ‘yes, we need to deindustrialise’. Renewable energy was incredibly expensive. The only really proven viable option to fossil fuel energy in the 1990s was nuclear energy, and nuclear energy was then, and still is, very, very expensive.
 
Going back to your question, Glenn, two really important things have changed: one is, people are living climate change now; the second thing is, the alternatives to fossil fuel energy are not only widely available and proven technologies, but they are also now the cheapest form of new energy. So I think there is a positive story to tell, but I think it is a shame it's taken us nearly 40 years to get here.
 
**Glenn**<br>
All right. Janaline. Well, that's a good mix of the good news and the bad news.
 
Now, on the back of what you were just talking about, it really sets the scene for us being in an interesting position. Or is it a bind? On the one hand, many of the drivers and technologies needed for decarbonization are advancing faster than expected: cold, hard economics, energy security and industrial competitiveness mean renewable energy continues to expand. That is all to the good.
 
On the other hand, the current pace of reduction still falls short of what scientists say is needed to meet the Paris Agreement's most ambitious goals. Climate politics and diplomacy has largely shifted from debating the reality of climate change to arguing over who should pay and how quickly fossil fuels should be phased down. The result is a world in which the energy transition is undeniably underway, but not yet moving with the speed, consistency or international unity needed to fully match the scale of the climate challenge.
 
So, Janaline, how is international climate diplomacy evolving and seeking to address the situation? What role is Australia playing in advancing or delaying the progress that is needed to avert the existential threat of uncontained climate change? And how has that role evolved since 1992?
 
**Janaline**<br>
Back in 1992, there was a lot of hope that an international convention could be negotiated that placed a bunch of obligations on countries to do certain things. That's how we would deal with climate change. It was very top down, and this was the model that had been very successful with the Montreal Protocol on Ozone-depleting Substances.
 
Why didn't it work on climate change? Because energy is the basis for all economic activity. The UN Convention on Climate Change set out a set of principles and, very importantly, the concept of common but differentiated responsibilities, which was code for ‘developed countries are the cause of this problem and the main contributor to it, and they should pay for it to be fixed, and developing countries should not be disadvantaged in that process’. That's not an unreasonable proposition.
 
So the Convention Parties agreed to negotiate a very specific Protocol similar to the Montreal Protocol, with very specific actions and specific targets. That system staggered through the Kyoto process, not helped by George W Bush famously pulling the US out of the Kyoto Protocol and John Howard then standing up and saying, ‘well, if the US is not in the Kyoto Protocol, Australia is also not going to ratify’. I was actually on the Australian climate negotiating team at the time and it was, I’ve got to say, a little demoralising, that we went from a situation where Australia had been a very active and serious player to being utterly sidelined: because our government had said it wasn't actually going to implement this thing, so nobody cared what we thought.
 
Subsequent to that, we have the attempts to negotiate the second commitment period under the Kyoto Protocol, where for the first time there was conversation about the big developing country emitters coming on board. By that stage, China, Brazil and India were emerging as very, very big emitters.
 
So then we have Copenhagen in 2009. That was supposed to be the agreement where we bring all of the major emitters on board, including the developing countries. And those negotiations very famously collapsed.
 
Another really significant political force in the international negotiations leading up to Copenhagen was a massive rearguard action by countries who were worried that the impact of the response measures to climate change in the developed world would lead to the collapse of their economies because people wouldn't be buying their fossil fuels anymore. So all of these economic interests, combined with a lack of real understanding amongst populations about the potentially catastrophic impacts of climate change made it really, really difficult to get that second agreement.
 
After the Copenhagen talks collapsed, there was a real sense that, at the end of the day, the big countries are just not going to cooperate to do something good for the world.
 
Now what had happened in Australia, is that we'd had a change of government and we had Kevin Rudd standing up as a new Prime Minister saying ‘climate change is the great moral issue of our time, we're going to deal with it’. And the first attempts to introduce an Australian economy wide price on carbon, which was the first in the world. So Australia went from not being a player to being a world leader.

After the collapse of Copenhagen, the Obama administration had already started to convene a thing that they called the Major Economies Forum, and this was a forum of the biggest emitters in the world. And the logic behind it was if we can get all of the biggest emitters into a room, and we can get them to talk sensibly about what they can each do, then that will actually deal with the bulk of the problem of climate change. Because even though countries like the Pacific island countries and the smaller African countries suffer a lot from the effects of climate change, there's not much that they can do within their own economies to fix it, because they're just not major emitters.
 
I think the Paris Agreement was a brilliant solution, because it was a change from that very top down approach that says ‘we'll all negotiate these things and then we'll just suck up the targets and we'll put up with this top down compliance regime’. It was: ‘OK, let's have a binding agreement where we all agree that we have to do something and we all agree to these basic principles’. But, because countries are best placed to understand what they can actually deliver, let's not give countries a number and say you have to meet this. Let's let these individual countries come up and say ‘here is what I think I can implement’.
 
Now this is a second best option, as you said, because obviously if you add up all of the commitments made under the Paris Agreement, they do fall short. But on the other hand, and this is sort of relevant to what I said in the context of the safeguard mechanism last week, there's no point in having these fantastic targets if you're not going to implement them. So instead of having the perfect solution, let's have the one that we can implement and then try to use that, to build on it to get to the place that we need to be.
 
So the Paris Agreement is completely different from the Kyoto Protocol. The Paris Agreement doesn't have a schedule of numbers attached to countries saying this is what you have to do. The Paris Agreement is built on the idea of nationally determined contributions. It is based on the idea that countries will come to the UN with a commitment outlining not just what they're committing to reduce, but how they are going to achieve that. And so in that context, we have commitments from China to reduce its emissions by 2016. We have commitments from most of the developed countries, including Australia, to achieve net zero emissions by 2050. We have a commitment from India to put on 450 gigawatts of new solar by, I think it was 2040. And just for context, that's nine times the Australian National Electricity Market in 2022.

Given that the Paris Agreement has these NDCs, the nationally determined contributions, what is the sort of diplomatic response when a country comes out with, perhaps, a contribution that might not be as good as other nations have? **Janaline**<br>
Well, I think the purpose of the nationally determined contribution is actually to have people make a commitment. And I think there is an understanding that sometimes countries aren't going to make quite lame commitments.
 
But I mean, it gets back to the power of international law: you can't force sovereign countries to do things they don't want to do. Countries will make comments, like, Pacific Island countries don't hold back if they think a country’s nationally determined contribution is insufficient. I don't know that you could call it diplomatic consequences. I mean, that again sounds a bit top down and the whole purpose of the Paris Agreement is to be a little bit more bottom up about what people are prepared to commit to. There is a political cost in terms of other countries' reactions if you're putting forward a really lame contribution.

**Janaline**<br>
So the Paris Agreement was signed in 2015, which set up this new process of nationally determined contributions. Clearly when you add them all up, they were not sufficient to meet the UN’s target of limiting global warming to no more than 1.5 degrees above pre industrial levels.
 
There was a huge push from environmental groups and also from low lying states, small island States, and particularly led by the Pacific, to have a global agreement to phase out fossil fuels. And this is obviously because the main source of climate-harming gases is the burning of fossil fuels.
 
Weirdly, the only Conference of Parties that has come close to talking about the need for a transition away from fossil fuels was in the UAE, which is of course a very major fossil fuel producer. And maybe it is not a coincidence that Sultan Al Jabar, who was the COP president at the time, was able to push through that agreement, because the UAE is a major fossil fuel producer and so he had the credibility to be able to say, ‘well, we want this to go forward’. Which is a million miles away from the position that a lot of the Middle Eastern states had taken in earlier years, where they were basically saying ‘you can't stop using fossil fuels because our economies depend on it’.
 
Since then, there has been this massive allergy to talking about phasing away fossil fuels. In Belém in Brazil, at COP 30 in 2025, the Brazilian presidency tried very, very hard to revive the idea of a just transition away from fossil fuels. There was a massive resistance not only to those words but even to referring back to the UAE Consensus, which is where those words had appeared. Now, one of the practices in international negotiations is that you can always go back to previously agreed language, and in this case even that was a massive struggle. So in the end there was a reference back to the UAE consensus, but it was a very vague reference and it was a huge struggle even to get that.
 
So why are countries so resistant to the idea of transitioning away from fossil fuels?
 
There is one narrative that says ‘it's because they're in the pockets of the fossil fuel companies. They just want to keep extracting this money for massive profits and they don't care about the state of the world’.
 
I think it's actually a bit more complex than that. So, unquestionably, throughout the last 36 years, fossil fuel companies have exerted enormous effort and invested millions and potentially billions of dollars into lobbying countries not to threaten their profits by taking climate action.
 
But these countries opposing the words around transitioning away from fossil fuels aren't doing it because they hate the planet; they are doing it because their economies are dependent on fossil fuels for their energy, and they need to be able to deliver reliable and affordable energy to their people. They need it for development. They need it to keep people alive. So I think there is actually a more benign explanation for this resistance.
 
So how do we deal with that? I reckon we deal with it by not talking about transitioning away from fossil fuels. By keeping that in mind as our objective, but by talking about ‘how do we get to transition away from fossil fuels’? And the way that we transition away from fossil fuels is to build the alternatives.
 
Now, as I said, renewable energy is now the cheapest form of new energy in the world. It's not just in Australia: according to the International Energy Agency, renewable energy is now the cheapest form of new generation.
